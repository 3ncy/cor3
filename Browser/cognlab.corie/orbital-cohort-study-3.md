# Do orbital children know what a plant looks like? We tested 340 kids. The results were uncomfortable.

*Dr. Sera Malk — Cognitive Development, Orbital Populations · 06/2251*

---

This is the third post in my ongoing series on cognitive development in children raised on orbital stations. If you want the methodology, start with post one. This one is about the finding that I've been sitting with for three months and wasn't sure how to write about.

## The study

We tested 340 children aged 6–14 across four orbital stations. The task was simple: we showed them images — some real photographs of plants, some high-quality VR renders from standard educational environments — and asked them to identify which was which, and to name the plant if they could.

For children who had spent their entire lives on-station (our 'station-born' cohort, about 60% of participants), the results were consistent across all four locations:

> | | |
> |-|-|
> |Recognition accuracy for VR renders|94%|
> |Recognition accuracy for real photographs|61%|
> |Naming accuracy for VR renders|78%|
> |Naming accuracy for real photographs|34%|

The station-born children were significantly better at identifying and naming the VR versions.

This is not surprising if you think about it for ten seconds. These children learned about plants through VR educational environments. The VR rose has consistent lighting, perfect symmetry, the exact colour that a curriculum designer decided a rose should be. The real rose photograph has shadows in the wrong places, wilted edges, colour variation. It doesn't look like the rose they learned.

## The part I wasn't prepared for

After the formal testing we ran an informal follow-up. We showed the children a small potted plant — a real one, brought on-station specifically for the study — and asked them to interact with it however they wanted.

Most of the station-born children were hesitant. Several asked if they could touch it. One child (age 9) asked my research assistant if the plant was 'a prop or a real file'. She meant: is this a physical object or a VR element she wasn't supposed to touch.

Two children cried. Not from distress — they said they weren't sad. They didn't have a better explanation.

One boy (age 11) held the pot for about four minutes without saying anything. When I asked what he was thinking he said:

> 'It smells different than I thought it would.'

I don't have a neat conclusion. The data is what it is. The curriculum designers did what made sense given the constraints. The children are fine, developmentally. They score normally on everything else.

But something is different. And I think we've decided, collectively, not to look at it too closely.

The full dataset will be submitted for peer review.

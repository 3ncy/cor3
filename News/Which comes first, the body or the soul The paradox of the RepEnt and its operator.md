## Where Does the Self End? RepEnts, Embodiment and the Boundaries of Identity.

“The body is not the prison of the soul. But neither, perhaps, is it its home.”

## Abstract

RepEnt technology presents one of the first substantial practical challenges to traditional theories of personal identity. While philosophical debates surrounding the relationship between mind and body have existed for centuries, the remote embodiment problem provides an unprecedented opportunity to examine those questions empirically. This paper argues that if personal identity follows conscious experience rather than biological continuity, then the current legal classification of RepEnts as mere property becomes philosophically unstable. Although this paper does not conclude that RepEnts themselves possess independent personhood, it argues that their status cannot be understood solely through existing categories of equipment, machinery or tools.

## Introduction

The invention of RepEnt technology has produced many legal, political, economic and philosophical questions. It has also revived one that philosophers believed had remained unanswered only because it could never truly be tested.

Let us ask: what exactly makes a human being?

For centuries, this question remained theoretical. With bionics and body alterations introduced into common practice, there was a brief renaissance of this philosophical concern, but nothing comparable to the questions faced today. Today, this question has become increasingly practical.

When an operator controls a RepEnt body remotely through a control system, their senses shift almost entirely into another biological organism, which they control almost as an extension of their own form. They see through different eyes and move limbs other than their own. If the relationship is not quite the same as the subconscious control one has over one’s own fingers, then it resembles the control of a perfected instrument. Many professional operators are, in this sense, artists using a tool as an extension of the self. But the difference is that a tool is not a body shell that seems animated by life when in use.

What, then, is the RepEnt relationship?

## Previous Literature

Questions concerning the relationship between body and consciousness are among the oldest in philosophy. Classical substance dualists, such as Rene Descartes, argued that the human is fundamentally composed of two distinct substances: the material body and the immaterial mind. Under this framework, the body serves as a vessel for the mind to interact with the physical world, but the body itself does not constitute the person.

Later, philosophers challenged this distinction. Materialist traditions argued that consciousness is an emergent property of physical processes within the brain, making personal identity inseparable from biological structure. Under this account, relocating consciousness outside its biological substrate would be impossible.

John Locke shifted the discussion away from physical substance entirely. Locke proposed that personal identity rests in the continuity of consciousness. A person remains the same individual insofar as they retain their memories, intentions and self-awareness across time. This argument became familiar and was popularised again with the spread of bionics and implants. Locke’s theory has become increasingly influential in discussions surrounding natural augmentation and cognitive transfer technologies.

Clark and Chalmers argue that cognitive processes may extend beyond the biological brain into external systems. While originally developed to explain tools and memory aids, the theory raises important questions regarding technologies that do not merely assist cognition but relocate sensory embodiment altogether.

RepEnt technology differs fundamentally from each of these historical discussions. What was always just a thought experiment has become reality. It introduces a practical situation in which a single conscious subject experiences the world through an alternative biological body while their original body remains psychologically alive.

## Central Argument

Premise 1: Personal identity follows conscious experience rather than the physical body through which that experience occurs.

Premise 2: The biological body left behind remains biologically human but no longer contains the location of the operator’s subjective experience.

Premise 3: If society accepts that the person continues to exist within the RepEnt during operation, then humanity cannot be defined solely by biological occupation of a particular body.

Premise 4: If humanity is not reducible to biological occupation, then the current legal definitions describing RepEnts solely as equipment become philosophically incomplete.

Conclusion: RepEnt technology challenges existing theories of personhood and demands reconsideration of the legal and moral status of remotely inhabited biological entities.
